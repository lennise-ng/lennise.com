What Malacca's traders knew about trust

A port that ran on credit between strangers, and what a 15th-century harbourmaster can teach a founder.

14 October 2026 · 1 minute read

For about a century, Malacca was the busiest port in Southeast Asia, and almost nobody who traded there spoke the same language as the person across the table. Ships arrived on one monsoon and left on the next. Between arrival and departure, a merchant from Gujarat had to sell to a buyer from Fujian, through a broker from Java, and be paid in a currency none of them had minted.

This should not have worked. It worked for a hundred years.

The four harbourmasters

The sultanate’s answer was not a law. It was a person. Each major trading community got its own shahbandar, a harbourmaster drawn from that community, who vouched for its merchants, settled their disputes, and was personally on the hook when they defaulted. Trust was outsourced to someone whose reputation could be destroyed by a single bad trade.

The system did not ask strangers to trust each other. It asked them to trust the same person.

If you have ever built a marketplace, you have rebuilt this. Escrow, verified sellers, reputation scores, dispute resolution — every one of them is a harbourmaster with better data. The interesting question is not whether the mechanism works. It is what the sultanate understood about why it works, and what we have forgotten.
