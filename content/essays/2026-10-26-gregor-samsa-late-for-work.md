---
title: Entry #002 – Gregor Samsa Is Going to Be Late for Work
subtitle: On Kafka, Jung, and what is left of us when the function is taken away.
date: 2026-10-26
tags: Arts & Culture · Literature · Psychology
---

Gregor Samsa wakes one morning to discover he has transformed into a gigantic, grotesque creature. Yet what is striking is that Gregor’s first reaction is not horror as to what has happened to his body. He worries that he will miss his train to work as he thinks about his manager. He worries his family would lose its source of income, even in a situation so absurd that his entire existence should have come to a halt.

Gregor immediately returns to his familiar function – the worker, the provider, the person who must be useful. As the story continues, the pain becomes increasingly clear. Once Gregor can no longer work and provide for his family, his position within the household changes as well. The man who once carried the family’s financial responsibility gradually becomes a burden.

Kafka was not writing a Jungian treatise on the persona. The persona, in Jung’s sense, is the face we build to be acceptable to the world: the role we present so that others know what to do with us.

But I think the story touches the fear remarkably close to it. If someone has invested too many years being known through what they do, what happens when they can no longer do it?

Gregor still has emotions, memories, love for his family, and an inner life. But all of these almost disappear behind the function through which everyone has become accustomed to seeing him. More tragically, Gregor himself has also grown accustomed in seeing himself that way.

Some people have been the strong one for so long that they don’t know what to do with their own vulnerability. Some are always the person who solves problems so whenever they need help themselves, they would feel ashamed. Others build their identity around success so completely that even a small failure makes them feel their entire worth has collapsed.

At this point, the persona no longer merely helps us adapt to circumstances. It begins to dictate what we are allowed to feel, express and even what we are allowed to admit about ourselves.

Kafka does not rescue him in the story. Gregor dies alone in his room, and what the family feels is not grief… but relief. They take a tram into the countryside, notice that the daughter has grown into a pretty young woman, and begin to think about finding her a husband. The function was replaced. The man was never missed.

Jung never said the persona was the problem. We need one. Nobody gets through a working day without a face to put on. The danger is forgetting that there is anyone behind it.

Nothing in Gregor’s family could have changed how they saw him. The only person who might have was Gregor – and he had stopped looking long before he woke up a creature.

So the question is not how to drop the mask. It is whether you know what is left when the function is taken away… and whether you would rather find out now, or the way Gregor did.
