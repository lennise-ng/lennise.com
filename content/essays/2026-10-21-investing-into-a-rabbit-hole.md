I invested in a company I initially struggled to understand. The deeper I went, the more it challenged one assumption I had always made about computing: that in order to use data, a computer has to see it.

Turns out, that assumption may not actually be true.

Over the next few essays, I’m going to try to explain what I’ve learned without needing a cryptography PhD.

We all know AI becomes more useful the more it knows about us. I think this is great… until it starts knowing things we’re not quite sure we want it to know.

Most of us are already comfortable giving AI some information. We ask it to rewrite emails, summarise documents, plan holidays, analyse spreadsheets, and occasionally act as an unpaid therapist at 2am.

But where do you draw the line?

The information that could make AI most useful is often exactly the information we’re least willing (and sometimes legally unable) to give it.

Banks want AI to understand financial behaviour without exposing customer records. Hospitals want AI to analyse patient data without compromising privacy. Governments want powerful AI without handing sensitive national data to outside infrastructure. Companies have the same problem with proprietary research, contracts and trade secrets.

So we seem to have created a strange trade-off. The more information we give AI, the more useful it becomes. But the more valuable that information is, the less comfortable we are giving it away.

Which raises a question I had honestly never thought about before: What if the computer didn’t actually need to see the data in order to use it?

That sounds ridiculous at first. How can a computer calculate something it cannot read?

Apparently, cryptographers have been thinking about exactly this problem for decades. There’s an entire field built around it called Fully Homomorphic Encryption, or FHE.

Very simply: it allows computation to happen on encrypted data without first turning that data back into readable information.

So I started asking different questions.

- What if a bank could analyse customer data without the computer actually seeing any of it?
- What if AI could work with your medical records without the infrastructure running it being able to read them?
- What if two organisations could learn from their combined datasets without handing each other the raw information?

That was the rabbit hole.

Trying to understand why something this useful has been so hard to make practical eventually led me to the founders of [ZipLogic](https://ziplogic.ai/).

Which is where things get much more interesting.
